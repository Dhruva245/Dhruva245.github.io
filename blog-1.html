FlexWalk: Our Journey from CAD to Its First Step

A 3D printed humanoid biped with 8 degrees of freedom, built from scratch with custom gearboxes, inverse kinematics and ZMP preview control.

Walking looks easy until you have to write it as code. Every step is a controlled fall. You lean forward, give up your balance for a moment, and catch yourself on a small patch of ground. People do this thousands of times a day without solving a single equation.

Every step we take is a controlled fall.

For a robot it is a lot harder, because there is no room for error. A wheeled robot or a four legged one has a stable base. A biped does not. A bit of gear backlash, one dropped serial packet or a sudden bump from the ground is enough to put it on the workbench.

We wanted to learn this from the ground up at the Society of Robotics and Automation (SRA), VJTI. We did not buy a humanoid kit, and we did not stop at simulation. We designed and built FlexWalk ourselves. It is about 50 cm tall and weighs about 2.6 kg, with 8 degrees of freedom. Each leg has four joints: hip pitch, hip roll, knee pitch and ankle pitch.

We also kept the hardware simple on purpose. FlexWalk runs on 8 Waveshare STS3215 serial bus servos (30 kg·cm peak torque at 12 V, 45 RPM, 55 g each, with 360° magnetic encoders), a Waveshare Bus Servo Adapter (A) talking over UART, and a lot of 3D printed PLA. Our bet was that careful mechanical iteration, proper kinematics and classic Zero Moment Point (ZMP) preview control would be enough to get a homemade biped standing and walking.

STEP 01Hardware and Mechanical Design

Each leg is built from seven main parts: the hip base on the torso frame, the hip pitch assembly, the hip roll assembly, the upper thigh link, the knee pitch housing, the calf link and the ankle pitch foot. Power comes from a bench supply (9 to 12.6 V DC) and the brain is a laptop running Python scripts. The laptop connects over USB to the Waveshare adapter, which turns our commands into half duplex UART at 1 Mbps for the servos.

From CAD Idealism to Version 2

Our first CAD models in Fusion 360 and Onshape looked great on screen. Version 1 fell apart as soon as we assembled it. We had connected the servos directly to the joints and used snap fit plastic clamps. Under load the PLA clamps bent, the servo shafts took too much bending force, and a few snap fits simply snapped while we were putting things together.

We needed more torque and less clutter. We first tried custom bevel gears for right angle drives, but they pushed hard along the axis and were too bulky to fit inside a 50 cm frame. So we switched to custom spur gear reductions:

  • Hip pitch, hip roll and knee pitch: 1.5:1 reduction, which brings the joint torque up to about 45 kg·cm.
  • Ankle pitch: 2:1 reduction, giving about 60 kg·cm for the heavy loads during stance and push off.

The other big change in Version 2 was getting rid of plastic rubbing on plastic. We printed ball bearing raceways straight into the joint housings and filled them with 4.5 mm steel BBs. That gave us smooth rotation, kept side loads off the servo output splines, saved some weight, and let us replace the snap clamps with solid motor plates held by M3 screws (aluminum and PLA).

STEP 02The Math Behind the Stride

The control code works like a pipeline. It takes a footstep goal and turns it into motor commands in these stages:

Command→Gait Generation→Foot Trajectory→Inverse Kinematics→Joint Angles→Servo Commands

Inverse Kinematics for a Two Link Leg

We treat each leg as a simple two link mechanism in the side view. The thigh l₁ and the calf l₂ are both 150 mm long. Given the hip position (xh, zh) and where we want the ankle to be (xa, za), the joint angles come from a few steps:

01 Find the distance D from the hip to the ankle:

D = √((xh − xa)² + (zh − za)²)

02 Solve the knee pitch angle θk (using the MuJoCo joint convention):

θk = arccos(l₁² + l₂² − D²2l₁l₂) − π

03 Solve the hip pitch angle θh (knee forward configuration):

γ = atan2(xh − xa, zh − za)
β = arccos(l₁² + D² − l₂²2l₁D)
θh = γ + β

04 Solve the ankle pitch angle θa so the foot stays parallel to the ground:

θa = −(θh + θk)

Foot Trajectory and Gait

The gait alternates between a swing phase (one foot in the air) and a stance phase (both feet down). We describe the swing with a normalized time τ = t / Ts, where 0 ≤ τ ≤ 1 and Ts is how long one step lasts. To keep the foot from scuffing the ground and to soften the impact at liftoff and touchdown, we use cycloidal and sine shaped paths:

Forward swing positionx(τ) = x₀ + L(τ − sin(2πτ)2π)
Lateral sway profiley(τ) = y₀ + Ay sin(πτ)
Vertical clearancez(τ) = z₀ + h sin(πτ)

Here L is the step length, h is how high the foot lifts (30 mm), and Ay is how far the hips sway sideways. The cycloid gives zero velocity and zero acceleration at τ = 0 and τ = 1, so there are no sudden jolts when the foot leaves or lands.

Balance with LIPM and ZMP Preview Control

For balance we model FlexWalk as a 3D Linear Inverted Pendulum (LIPM). All of the mass sits at one point, the center of mass (CoM), at height zc. Stability is then described by the Zero Moment Point (ZMP):

px = xCoM − zcg ẍCoM
py = yCoM − zcg ÿCoM

ZMP preview control looks ahead at where the next footsteps will be. It solves an optimal control problem and gives a smooth CoM path (xCoM, yCoM) that moves our weight over the stance foot before the swing foot leaves the ground.

STEP 03Sim to Real in MuJoCo

Before touching the hardware we wanted a good physics simulation. We first tried the Onshape to Robot tool to turn our CAD into a URDF, but we hit account rate limits and the exporter stopped working. Instead of waiting, we wrote the MuJoCo XML model ourselves from scratch.

Writing the XML by hand meant defining every joint offset, inertia tensor, collision shape, position actuator and gear ratio, and loading our own STL meshes for the visuals. It was slow, but it taught us exactly how FlexWalk is put together and where its mass sits.

Our tuning loop looked like this:

Robot Model→Preview Controller→Trajectory Generator→Simulation→Evaluation

In MuJoCo we tested static balance, weight shifts onto one leg, step responses and full gait cycles. Seeing FlexWalk walk across the simulated floor gave us the confidence to move to the real robot.

MURPHY'S LAWWhat Broke Along the Way

No robot goes from CAD to reality without something breaking. Hardware shows you every assumption you made in simulation, and it is not gentle about it.

ISSUE 01
Overnight print failures and tolerance fights

Printing thin, load bearing links in PLA is a constant fight with warping and bed adhesion. We had 10 hour prints that curled at the corners or turned into a pile of plastic spaghetti overnight. Pulling support material out of narrow bearing channels also damaged several links, so we started splitting complex parts into smaller sub assemblies that print in a better orientation.

ISSUE 02
The brass insert trap

We first held the frame plates with double sided knurled heat set brass inserts. Under joint loads and screw torque, the straight knurls ripped through the melted PLA and the inserts spun freely in their pockets. The fix was flanged, flat base inserts pushed in from the side that takes the load, so they actually stay put.

ISSUE 03
Too many bearings and tight clearances

Our 3D printed ball bearings had their own problems. Too many 4.5 mm steel BBs in the raceway made them jam and drag, so we had to find the exact ball count that turns smoothly. The ankle spur gear also would not mesh until we added a spacer cylinder between the shaft and the calf link, and we had to trim the calf link chamfers that were blocking the knee from bending fully.

ISSUE 04
A skipped gear and a broken motor mount

During a real step sequence, an ankle spur gear skipped a tooth under impact. That left the encoder and the actual joint out of sync, and the servo drove itself hard into its mechanical stop. The torque snapped the 3D printed motor mount plate right away. It was a clear reminder that clean kinematics in simulation tell you very little about impact loads in real life.

ISSUE 05
Wiring, bus routing and servo settings

Running eight servos on one serial bus takes careful wiring. Loose jumper leads on the adapter board kept dropping packets in the middle of a step. We fixed that with custom wiring harnesses and strain relief loops across the moving joints, gave each servo its own SCS ID from 1 to 8 using the Feetech setup tools, and set torque limits and velocity curves per joint. The ankle pitch servos get the most torque and the knee pitch servos get less.
